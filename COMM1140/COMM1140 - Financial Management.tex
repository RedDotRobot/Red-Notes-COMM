\section{Introduction to Financial Management}
	\textbf{Learning Objectives}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Differentiate between accounting, finance, and tax
			\item Understand the different forms of accounting information
			\item Differentiate between revenues and expenses
			\item Explain the concepts of accrual accounting, cash accounting, and accounting profit
		\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{What is financial management?}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Financial management is the process of planning, organising, controlling, and monitoring financial resources to achieve company goals and objectives $\rightarrow$ how we use information to make informed business decisions
			\item The \textit{primary objective} of financial management is to maximise shareholder value through appropriate utilisation and decision making
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Why is financial management important to study}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Financial management is a key life skill
			\item Entrepreneurship and roles within a company
			\item Implications on all aspects of business operations (such as Marketing, Management, Tax, Information Systems, Actuary)
			\item Eg. Budgeting within a business
			\item Measuring performance of a business, eg. profit
		\end{itemize}

		\textbf{Accounting is how you understand and run a successful business}

		Signs of business health include
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Profitability
				\item Cash flow
				\item Short and long term sustainability
				\item Business value
			\end{itemize}
		
		Accounting powers every business decision, categorised as long-term (strategic), short-term (operational), commercial (evaluation of business deals). Ultimately, accounting enables career success, as an advisor, entrepreneur, or a leader. Accounting is a good pathway into a career, as a way of entering a company. \\
		
		Finance helps you value a business and make good investment decisions using accounting information.

			\item Accounting ensures that high-quality numbers are available for analysis
			\item Finance needs accounting to begin the process of \colorbox{pastel_green}{valuation} (ie. to estimate the value or price of companies/assets/new projects)
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Accounting and Tax}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Tax is the regulatory guidebook that ensures financial responsibility and compliance for a successful business
			\item It involves understanding and applying tax laws to ensure compliance, minimise tax liabilities, and take advantage of available deductions
			\item Tax calculations draw directly on accounting information
			\item It is an important part of financial management in minimising liabilities, leverage deductions, and contributing ethically to government revenue
		\end{itemize}
	\subsection{Accounting Information}
		\subsubsection{Who uses accounting information?}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item A wide range of stakeholders use accounting information daily
				\item These users often have different needs that accounting information can satisfy
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{What are the different types of information in accounting}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Financial accounting $\rightarrow$ Information given to people \colorbox{pastel_red}{outside} of the enterprise
				\item Management accounting $\rightarrow$ Information given to users \colorbox{pastel_blue}{within} the enterprise to assist in operational planning and controlling decisions
				\item Audit and internal control $\rightarrow$ An audit confirms the accuracy of financial statements and internal control involves implementing policies and procedures that safeguard assets and ensure reliable financial information
				\item Social and environmental accounting (accounting for sustainability) $\rightarrow$ Provision of non-financial information to users \colorbox{pastel_red}{external} to the enterprise 
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Financial accounting information and decision-making}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item \textbf{Financial accounting} primarily serves \colorbox{pastel_red}{external stakeholders}, such as investors/shareholders, creditors, regulatory bodies and the general public
				\item It provides an overview of a company's financial health
				\item Analysing financial statements allows managers to evaluate profitability, liquidity, and solvency
				\item Investors rely on financial statements to inform their investment decisions
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Management accounting information and decision-making}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item \textbf{Management accounting} is designed for \colorbox{pastel_blue}{internal stakeholders}, including managers and executives
				\item It provides detailed financial information to aid in strategic planning, budgeting, performance evaluation, and internal decision-making
				\item Cost accounting, a subset of management accounting, allows managers to optimise pricing and cost efficiency
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Audit and internal control information and decision-making}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item \textbf{Audits and internal control systems} ensure the reliability and integrity of financial inform, instilling confidence in decision-makers
				\item Audits and internal control systems can also benefit internal stakeholders by safeguarding assets and promoting operational efficiency
				\item Investors trust audited financial statements
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Social and environmental accounting information and decision-making}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Social and environmental accounting caters to both internal and external stakeholders
				\item Internally, it helps management assess the company's impact on environmental and societal sustainability
				\item Externally, it informs socially conscious investors, customers, and regulatory bodies about the company's commitment to social and environmental responsibility
			\end{itemize}


	\subsection{Revenue and Expenses}

		\subsubsection{Revenue}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Revenue represents an increase in company wealth
				\item Wealth increases because customers:
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Pay cash for goods or services
						\item Promise to pay cash (accounts receivable)
					\end{itemize}
				\item Types of revenue include:
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Sales revenue $\rightarrow$ Generated by selling goods and/or services in the ordinary course of business
						\item Other revenue may consist of items such as:
							\begin{itemize}
								\item Interest income on bank accounts or investments (passive revenue)
								\item Dividends received from investments in other companies (dividends are optional payments to shareholders from the profit of the company as a "thank-you" payment)
							\end{itemize}
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Expenses}

			\begin{itemize}
				\item Expenses represent decreases in company wealth
				\item Expenses must be incurred in order to earn revenue
				\item These \textbf{do not} include the payment of dividends since these are "distributions" of net profit and are \colorbox{pastel_red}{not} mandatory
			\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Cash Accounting}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Cash accounting involves recording revenues and expenses \textbf{at the time the cash is received or paid}
			\item This is reasonably precise given that the accountant knows whether cash has been paid or received, and the amount is easily determined
		\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Limitations of cash accounting}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item The complexity of business means that the financial health of a company is affected by transactions and cash flows in both the past and future
				\item Events where the \textbf{timing} of cash flows differs from the \textbf{substance} of the transaction include:
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Sale of goods or services on credit
						\item Services that will be paid in a later period (eg. electricity, water, gas, hotel accommodations)
						\item Companies receiving cash in advance for services provided (eg. magazine subscriptions, airline tickets, concert tickets)
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Accrual Accounting}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item To cope with these complexities, most businesses use accrual accounting
			\item Accrual accounting involves recording revenues and expenses \textbf{at the time they occur}, not when the cash is received or paid
			\item In the income statement, revenues and expenses are recognised when an economically meaningful event has occurred
			\item The \colorbox{pastel_green}{key test for revenue recognition is whether the goods and services have been \textbf{rendered}} (ie. delivery to a customer or provision of a service)
			\item \textbf{Receipt of cash is not required for revenue to be recognised}
			\item Equipment used for multiple years is not considered an expense, rather an asset. The depreciation of the asset is the expense
		\end{itemize}

			\centering
			\setstretch{1.25}

				\multicolumn{1}{c|}{} &

				\multicolumn{1}{c}{\textbf{Accrual basis}} \\
				\hline
				\textbf{Record revenues when:} & Cash is received 	& Revenues are earned \\
				\textbf{Record expenses when:} & Cash is paid		& Expenses are incurred \\

				\textbf{Benefits:}									& Simple and effective in managing the cash flow position of a business				& More accurate and clear view of the financial health of the business \\
				\hline

\section{Understanding Financial Statements}
	
	\textbf{Learning Objectives}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Understand how accounting information is presented through the financial statements
			\item Describe the relationship between the balance sheet and the income statement (P \& L)
			\item Identify the components of the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement
			\item Prepare in income statement and balance sheet
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{How is financial accounting information presented}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Financial accounting information is presented in an annual report
			\item The annual report is a comprehensive document produced by a company at the end of its financial year
			\item It serves as a detailed account of the company's financial performance, operational activities, and overall position during the preceding year
			\item Annual reports can be obtained from either
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Company websites
					\item Stock exchanges
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Key financial statements}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Balance sheet
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Financial position of a company \colorbox{pastel_red}{at a particular point in time}
					\item Company's set of financial resources and obligations at a point in time
					\item It shows a company's resources (assets) and how they were financed (liability + equity)
				\end{itemize}
			\item Income statement
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Financial performance of a company over a period of time
					\item How effect a company is at generating a profit from operations \colorbox{pastel_red}{over a period of time}
					\item It shows the revenues and expenses incurred over a period of time
				\end{itemize}
			\item Cash flow statement
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Measures cash inflows (sources of cash) and cash outflows (uses of cash) \colorbox{pastel_red}{over a period of time}
					\item Prepared using cash accounting
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{The Balance Sheet}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item In Australia, the end of the financial year is 30 June since it aligns with the tax year end
				\item The balance sheet has three main elements:
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Assets
							\begin{itemize}
								\item Resources that will be used by the company this year or in future years
								\item Eg. cash, accounts receivable, property, equipment, inventory, investments
								\item Classified as:
									\begin{itemize}
										\item \colorbox{pastel_green}{Current} (short-term) assets expected to be used in the next 12 months, eg. cash, accounts receivable, inventory
										\item \colorbox{pastel_green}{Non-current} (long-term) assets that will be used for longer than 12 months, eg. property, plant and equipment, buildings, land
									\end{itemize}
							\end{itemize}
						\item Liabilities
							\begin{itemize}
								\item A list of debt and other financial obligations (what the company owes)
								\item Eg. accounts payable, loan payable, income taxes payable
								\item Classified as:
									\begin{itemize}
										\item \colorbox{pastel_green}{Current,} eg. accounts payable, wages payable
										\item \colorbox{pastel_green}{Non-current} liabilities that will extend passed a year, eg. loans payable
									\end{itemize}
							\end{itemize}
						\item Shareholder's Equity (also known as Owner's Equity)
							\begin{itemize}
								\item What belongs to the owners, the "net assets" of the company (what would be left if all liabilities were paid for and all assets sold \colorbox{pastel_purple}{($SE = A-L$))}
								\item Consists of:
									\begin{itemize}
										\item Share capital
										\item Retained profits
									\end{itemize}
							\end{itemize}
					\end{itemize}
				\item \colorbox{pastel_red}{Note:} the sum of assets on a Balance Sheet must always equal the sum of total liabilities and shareholder's equity
			\end{itemize}

			\begin{question}{Example}
				\begin{align*}
					\text{Assets} &= \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity} 			\\
					\textit{(Resources)} &\qquad \textit{(Sources)}					\\
					\$30,000 &= \$18,000 + \$12,000									\\
							 & \text{Bought furniture for \$10,000 with a loan}		\\
					\$10,000 &= \$10,000											\\
					\$40,000 &= \$28,000 + \$12,000									\\
							 & \text{Or}											\\
					\text{Assets} &- \text{Liabilities} = \text{Equity}				\\
					\$40,000 &- \$28,000 = \$12,000
				\end{align*}
			\end{question}
		
		\subsubsection{Income Statement}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Displays financial performance derived from business operations over a specific time period
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Reports revenues earned, and any expenses incurred
						\item \textbf{Revenue}: inflows of economic benefits that increase Shareholder's Equity
						\item \textbf{Expenses}: Use or loss of economic benefits that decrease Shareholder's Equity
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

				You give a lecture in February for \$3,000. You receive the cash in March. You pay \$1,000, in February, to hire the room.

		\subsubsection{Statement of Cash Flows}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item The cash flow statement provides details of movements in a company's cash balance over a specific time period
				\item These are categorised into:
					\begin{enumerate}
						\item \textbf{Operating activities:} main revenue-producing activities
						\item \textbf{Investing activities:} acquisition and disposal of long-term assets
						\item \textbf{Financing activities:} equity capital and borrowings
					\end{enumerate}
				\item Prepared using cash accounting
			\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Connecting the Income Statement and the Balance Sheet}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Retained profits} represent the sum of all previous profits minus any dividends paid to shareholders
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Dividends are a distribution of the profits as a reward for investing in the company
				\end{itemize}
			\item Share capital is defined as the money invested in the business in return for shares
		\end{itemize}

\section{Analysing Business Transactions}

	\textbf{Learning Objectives}

		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Identify and record business transactions using transaction analysis
			\item Understand how different business transactions impact the financial statements
			\item Identify how accrual adjustments impact the financial statements
		\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{What are business transactions?}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Transactions are events that affect the operations or finances of an organisation
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Examples: selling goods or services, paying employees, paying dividends
				\end{itemize}
			\item Transaction analysis involves examining each business business transaction with the aim of understanding its effect on the accounting equation ($A = L + SE$)
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Recording transactions using the accounting equation}

		\begin{question}{Example}
			\textbf{Revenues + Expenses}

				\underline{In March:}
				\begin{enumerate}
					\item You give a training course for \$5,000 in cash
					\item You pay \$1,000 rent for the room
				\end{enumerate}
				\begin{alignat*}{2}
					\text{Assets} \quad &= \quad \text{Liabilities} \quad &&+ \quad \text{Equity}	\\
					\text{Cash } + 5,000 \quad &= &&+ \quad 5,000	\\
					\text{Cash } (1,000) \quad &= && \qquad (1,000)
				\end{alignat*}

				Hence, profit is \$4,000.
				\\

			\textbf{Equity} 

				\begin{table}[H]
					\begin{center}
						\setstretch{1.25}
						\begin{tabular}[c]{p{8cm}|p{3cm}}
							\hline
							Statement of Retained Profit				& \$		\\
							\hline
							Opening Retained Profit (1st March)			& 10,000	\\
							\qquad Profit 								& 4,000		\\
							\qquad - Dividends							& (3,000)	\\
							Closing Retained Profit (31st March)		& 11,000	\\
							\hline
						\end{tabular}
					\end{center}
				\end{table}
			
		\end{question}

		\begin{alignat*}{3}
			\text{Assets} \quad &= \quad \text{Liabilities} \quad &&+ \quad \text{Shareholder's Equity}	\\
			A \quad &= \quad L \quad &&+ \quad SE	\\
			CA + NCA \quad &= \quad CL + NCL \quad &&+ \quad SC + RP
		\end{alignat*}

		Where:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $CA + NCA = \text{Current Assets} + \text{Non-Current Assets}$
			\item $CL + NCL = \text{Current Liabilities} + \text{Non-Current Liabilities}$
			\item $SE = SC + Op. \; RP + \text{Profits} - \text{Dividends}$
			\item $SC = \text{Capital contributions by equity holders (Share Capital)}$
			\item $RP = \text{Retained Profits} = Op.\;RP + \text{Profit} - \text{Dividends}$
				\begin{itemize}
					\item $Op. \; RP = \text{Opening Retained Profits}$
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Accrual Accounting Adjustments}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Accrual accounting requires revenues and expenses to be recognised in the accounting period to which they relate (ie. when they occur)
			\item This may require an accrual adjustment to be made on the last day of the financial period to bring records up to date
		\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Unearned revenues (deferred revenues)}

			\begin{itemize}
				\item You are a Seller (receiver of cash)
				\item Cash is received in advance of earning revenue
					\begin{itemize}
						\item The recognition of revenue is deferred
					\end{itemize}
				\item \colorbox{pastel_red}{A liability account}
				\item Liability becomes revenue when goods or services owing are provided
				\item Examples
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Cash deposit for work to be carried out in the future
						\item Airline and concert tickets
						\item Video streaming subscriptions
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Prepayments}
		
			\begin{itemize}
				\item You are a Customer (cash payer)
				\item Cash paid in advance of incurring expense
					\begin{itemize}
						\item There is still value extending into the future
					\end{itemize}
				\item Examples
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Prepaid insurance
						\item Prepaid rent
						\item Office supplies
					\end{itemize}
				\item \colorbox{pastel_green}{An asset account}
				\item Once the asset is used, the expense is incurred and an adjustment is made
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Accrued revenues (receivables)}

			\begin{itemize}
				\item You are a Seller
				\item Revenue has been earned, but cash not received
				\item \colorbox{pastel_green}{An asset account}
				\item Examples
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Credit sales
						\item Commissions earned but not received
						\item Interest earned on an investment but not received
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Accrued expenses (payables)}

			\begin{itemize}
				\item You are a Customer
				\item Expenses are incurred, but the cash is not paid
				\item \colorbox{pastel_red}{A liability account}
				\item Examples
					\begin{itemize}

	\subsection{Depreciation of non-current assets}
		
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Depreciation involves allocating the cost of a non-current asset over its lifespan (useful like) to acknowledge the gradual consumption of the asset's economic value as an expense
			\item Accumulated Depreciation (Balance Sheet account) shows all depreciation charged against an asset to date
			\item Depreciation Expense (Income Statement account) shows only this year's depreciation allocation
			\item Net Book Value: $\text{Original Purchase Cost} - \text{Accumulated Depreciation}$
		\end{itemize}

		\begin{question}{Example}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Equipment costs \$10,000 with a useful life of 4 years and no estimated salvage value
				\item The \textbf{straight-line depreciation method} is applied throughout the equipment's useful life, reflecting the assumption that the asset will be utilised evenly over its lifespan
				\item $\text{Depreciation calculation} = \$10,000 / 4 \text{ years } = \$2,500$
				\item After 3 years, the book value of the equipment is:
			\end{itemize}
			\begin{table}[H]
				\begin{center}
					\setstretch{1.25}
					\begin{tabular}[c]{p{8cm}p{3cm}}
						Equipment							& \$10,000	\\
						Less: Accumulated depreciation		& (\$7,500)	\\
						Book value							& \$2,500	\\
					\end{tabular}
				\end{center}
			\end{table}
		\end{question}

			\begin{align*}
				\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Share Price}}{\text{Earnings Per Share (EPS)}}

		\subsubsection{Dividend Yield}

\section{Free Cash Flow Estimation}

	\subsection{What is Free Cash FLow (FCF)}
		
		FCF is the cash left after operating the business and reinvesting in it. FCF can be used to pay dividends, pay down debt, or just sit as cash (for later reinvestment). It provides information about a company's financial strength, flexibility, and quality of earnings. Absolute valuation is when a firm is valued based on the cash it will generate for investors Absolute valuation is when a firm is valued based on the cash it will generate for investors.

		\begin{align*}
			\text{FCF} =& \text{NOPAT}	\\
						&+ \text{Depreciation & Amortisation}	\\
						&- \text{Capital Expenditures}	\\
						&- \text{Change on Op. Net Working Capital}
		\end{align*}

		NOPAT is EBIT $\times (1 - \text{Tax Rate})$, and since depreciation is tax deductible it must be removed for tax to be calculated. However, since it is not a cash flow, it is added back when calculating FCF. The only cash flow effect of deducting depreciation is to reduce taxes.

		Terminal cash flow is the value that the firm could be sold for at the horizon period. It is the single value that represents all the infinite FCF that come after the horizon period. This is calculated using firm multiples.

\section{Business Taxation}
	
	\subsection{What is a tax?}
		
		OECD defines tax as "a compulsory, unrequited payment to general government. Taxes are unrequited in the sense that benefits provided by government to taxpayers are not normally in proportion to their payments".

	\subsection{Why do governments impose taxation?}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item The purpose of taxation is to raise revenue to fund the provision of public goods and services
			\item They solve market failures
			\item Helps in social/community goals: distribution objectives, inequality
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Tax policy objectives}

		What makes a 'good' tax
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Simple: easy to understand and comply with
			\item Equitable: the burden should be borne fairly
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Vertical equity: tax the rich
					\item Horizontal equity: tax people with same income equally
				\end{itemize}
			\item Efficient: should not distort decision making
			\item Sustainable: structurally durable, yet flexible to deal with changes
			\item Consistent: tax policy should be internally consistent
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Administration of Australia's tax system}
		
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Federal - Australian Taxation Office (ATO), State - Office of State Revenue (OSR)
			\item Commissioner of Taxation
			\item Inspector-General of Taxation & Taxation Ombudsman (IGTO)
			\item Administrative Review Tribunal (ART)
			\item Taxpayers bear the responsibility for correctly preparing tax returns
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Sources of Australian taxation law}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Statute law - legislation
			\item Case law - 'common law'
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Created by the decisions of courts
					\item Accumulators over time
					\item Creation of law to fill a legislative vacuum
					\item Statutory interpretation
				\end{itemize}
			\item ATO practice - a de facto source of law
				\begin{itemize}
					\item ATO's assessment and review procedures, day-to-day decisions in formal/informal dealings
					\item Not technically law, but creates informal law
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}
		
	\subsection{Who must pay income tax?}

		In Australia, income tax is payable by each individual and company (and other entities).
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Unlike other countries, no 'joint filing' or 'family filing' - every individual must pay separately
			\item Requirement to ledge annual tax return: s161(1) \textit{Income Tax Assessment Act} 1936 (ITAA1936)
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{What happens if taxes are not paid?}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item There are ways of legitimately and illegitimately reducing taxes
			\item Large businesses are able to hire advisors to minimise their tax whereas small businesses who cannot afford such services are more likely to engage in illegitimate tax reduction
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Tax evasion, avoidance, and planning}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Tax evasion}: involves the illegal underpayment of tax, typically by failing to disclose liabilities or wrongly claiming benefits
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Very illegal
				\end{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Tax avoidance}: involves entering into arrangements that are designed to exploit unintended defects and loopholes in the law
				\begin{itemize}
					\item There are anti tax avoidance legislations that account for many such cases
					\item Is still legal
				\end{itemize}
			\item \textbf{Tax planning}: involves the arrangement of one's affairs to exploit legitimate opportunities available under the law
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{What happens if taxes are not paid?}

		Consequences of unacceptable or overly aggressive tax management:
			
			\begin{itemize}
				\item Reputational effects - companies want to be seen as being 'good corporate citizens'
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Eg. Starbucks in the UK suffered heavy reputational consequences after it was discovered that they didn't pay taxes to the UK
					\end{itemize}
				\item Companies need to demonstrate they pay their 'fair share' of taxes ('social contract')
				\item ATO audits and/or audit penalties
				\item Imprisonment
			\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Types of taxpayers}

		In Australia, different types of taxpayers are treated in different ways
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Individuals
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Employment income, investment income, deductions (from tax claims)
					\item Taxed at personal marginal tax rates
				\end{itemize}
			\item Sole trader
				\begin{itemize}
					\item An individual conducting a business in their own name
					\item Not legally different from the individual, however will have more deductions
				\end{itemize}
			\item Partnership
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Two or more people conducting a business together as partners
					\item It is treated as a conduit between the people, tax is distributed between shareholders
				\end{itemize}
			\item Trust
				\begin{itemize}
					\item A legal relationship where a trustee manages assets on behalf of the beneficiaries
					\item Not treated as a separate taxpayer
					\item Treated as a conduit for tax purposes
				\end{itemize}
			\item Company
				\begin{itemize}
					\item A separate legal entity
					\item Treated as a separate taxpayer from shareholders and must lodge a tax return
					\item Taxed at a company tax rate of 30\% or 25\% for small companies (aggregate turnover below \$50m).
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Company income tax}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item There is a fundamental difference between income tax calculated in the annual financial statements and income tax liability calculated in the annual income tax return
			\item Financial statements are prepared in accordance with accounting standards (IFRS)
				\begin{itemize}
					\item 'Income tax expense' in the Income Statement
					\item 'Income tax liability' in the Balance Sheet
					\item 'Cash taxes paid' in the Cashflow Statement
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}
		\begin{question}{Formula for tax}
			\begin{align*}
				\text{Taxable income} &= \text{Assessable income} - \text{Deductions} \quad \left[\text{s4-15 ITAA1197}\right]	\\
				\text{Tax payable} &= \left(\text{Taxable income} \times \text{Relevant rate}) - \text{Tax offsets} \quad \left[\text{s4-10 ITAA1997}\right]
			\end{align*}
		\end{question}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Tax offsets are different to deductions
			\item Since they reduce tax payable, they are more valuable
			\item Eg. Accounts for tax paid overseas
		\end{itemize}
		\begin{align*}
			\text{Assessable income} = \text{Ordinary income} + \text{Statutory income} \quad \left[\text{s6-1(1) ITAA1997}\right]
		\end{align*}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Ordinary income is income according to ordinary concepts
			\item Statutory income is amounts that are not ordinary income but are included in assessable income
		\end{itemize}

		\subsubsection{Deductions}
			\begin{itemize}
				\item $\text{Deductions} = \text{costs incurred by the taxpayer in gaining or producing their assessable income}$
				\item Specific deductions are amounts that a provision of the Act allows you to deduct
				\item Bad debts can be deducted
				\item In accounting, doubtful debts can we written off however in taxation they must be bad
				\item General deductions can be made for any loss or outgoing to the extent that:
					\begin{itemize}
						\item It is incurred in gaining or producing assessable income
							\begin{itemize}
								\item There is maximum value of a good as long as it serves a business related function
							\end{itemize}
						\item It is necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing your assessable income
						\item Cannot deduct the following
							\begin{itemize}
								\item Loss or outgoing of capital
								\item Loss or outgoing of a private or domestic nature
								\item It is incurred in relation to gaining or producing your exempt income
								\item A provision of this Act prevents you from deducting it
							\end{itemize}
						\item Depreciation can be claimed
						\item 'Capital allowances' is a generic term that refers to the taxation treatment of depreciable assets
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item If a business experiences losses (eg. COVID), they are eligible to hold their taxes and pay them in a later year (carried forward tax loss)
		\item In this case, the business must pass either the same business or similar business tests (shareholders with >50\% voting power must have their shares at the start and end of the year)
	\end{itemize}

\section{Audit and Internal Control}

	Internal controls are safeguards that help a company achieve its goals and protect its assets. They are designed to prevent errors, fraud, and misuse of resources by establishing guidelines and processes that ensure accountability, accuracy, and compliance with laws and regulations.
	\\ \\
	\textbf{Technical definition}: Internal control is a process effected by an entity's board of directors, management, and other personnel, designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in the following categories:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Effectiveness and efficiency of operations including safeguarding assets against loss
		\item Reliability of internal and external financial and non-financial reporting
		\item Compliance with applicable laws and regulations
	\end{itemize}

	Note that it is impossible to obtain absolute assurance, since there are always unforeseeable future events

	\subsection{Components of internal controls}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Control environment
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Refers to the overall attitude, awareness, and actions of an organisation towards establishing and maintaining effective control measures
					\item A strong control environment is characterised by management's emphasis on integrity, ethical behaviour, and accountability
					\item This promotes a culture of compliance, where employees understand and adhere to established policies and procedures
				\end{itemize}
			\item Risk assessment
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Identifying risks that could impact the business
					\item Assessing the likelihood of these risks
					\item Evaluation of the impacts
					\item Prioritise risks that require immediate action
					\item Develop risk management strategies
				\end{itemize}
			\item Control activities
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Actions or steps taken by an organisation to mitigate the effects of key risks identified during risk assessment
					\item This often involves the segregation of duties and authorisation and approval processes
				\end{itemize}
			\item Information and communication
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Making sure the right information reaches the right people at the right time
					\item How management communicate risk management policies to staff
					\item How management seek feedback from staff on concerns and issues that require attention
					\item A good system creates an environment that allows and promotes free flowing information from the bottom up
				\end{itemize}
			\item Monitoring
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Periodically checking up on security measures
					\item Tracking metrics relevant to internal controls
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Limitations}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Genuine mistakes
			\item Collusion among employees (not very often)
			\item Computer fraud
			\item Cost versus benefit of internal control systems (eg. unprofitable to hire infinitely many security guards)
		\end{itemize}

	
	\subsection{Auditing}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Expression of an opinion on the financial statements
			\item Provide a true and fair view
			\item In accordance with Corporation Act 2001
			\item The auditor's report is included in the annual report
		\end{itemize}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Auditors are independent of the company, and can therefore be an unbiased, professionally sceptical reviewer of the financial statements
		\end{itemize}
		
		\subsubsection{Types of audit opinions}

			\begin{itemize}
				\item Unmodified (or unqualified) opinion
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Issued when the auditors believe that the financial statements give a true and fair view
						\item Absence of qualifying language
					\end{itemize}
				\item Qualified opinion
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Issued when a specific part of the financial statements contains a material misstatement or adequate evidence cannot be obtained in a specific, material area, and the rest of the financial statements are found to present a true and fair view, in accordance with accounting standards
					\end{itemize}
				\item Adverse opinion
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Auditors believe that the misstatements are so pervasive that the financial statements do not present a true and fair view
					\end{itemize}
				\item Disclaimer opinion
					\begin{itemize}
						\item Auditors are unable to express an opinion because of a limitation in the work the auditors were able to do
					\end{itemize}
			\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Section 2 - Financial Statement Preparation}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Balance sheets
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Each transaction is pretty self explanatory
					\item Make sure that all transactions follow the equation (it may be helpful to mark the sections on the provided balance sheet)
						\begin{align*}
							\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}
						\end{align*}
					\item Each step should in itself follow this rule for transactions with multiple steps
					\item For things like rent and insurance that might not be paid at the beginning of the financial year, subtract the total from cash and add the total amount under "Prepaid ...". Then deduct the used amount from the "Prepaid ..." and the retained profits column.
					\item Accounts receivable is sometimes abbreviated as "A/Rec." or something similar
					\item Note that inventory is NOT the same as COGS. 
				\end{itemize}
			\item Income statements
				\begin{itemize}
					\item Income statements are often a lot less structured, but should follow the following layout
						\begin{table}[H]
							\setstretch{1.25}
							\centering
							\begin{tabular}[c]{p{5cm}|p{5cm}}
								\hline
										& \multicolumn{1}{c}{\$}		\\
								\hline
								Sales Revenue	&	\\
								LESS: COGS	&	\\
								\textbf{Gross Profit}	&	\\
								\hline
								Less expenses:	&	\\
								\qquad Wages	&	\\
								\qquad Rent	&	\\
								\qquad Depreciation	&	\\
								\qquad Insurance	&	\\
								\hline
								Net profit & \\
								\hline
							\end{tabular}
						\end{table}
						
					\item Everything other than the section below "Less expenses:" is guaranteed to have that layout, so memorise it
					\item Gross profit refers to $\text{Revenue} - \text{COGS}$ whereas net profit includes all additional costs of a company
				\end{itemize}
			\item Closing/retained profits
				\begin{itemize}
					\item This can be given as a table or just a calculation
					\item Simply, it is the retained profits from the previous year (given), add the net profit for the period, less dividends declared during the year
						\begin{table}[H]
							\setstretch{1.25}
							\centering
							\begin{tabular}[c]{p{8cm}|m{4cm}}
								\hline
								& \multicolumn{1}{c}{\$}	\\
								\hline
								Retained profits at (end of previous financial year)	&	\\
								ADD: net profit for the period		&	\\
								LESS: dividends declared during the period &	\\
								\hline
								Retained profits at (end of finished financial year)	&	\\
								\hline
							\end{tabular}
						\end{table}
					\item These questions usually require you to show working, just writing ADD and LESS is sufficient
				\end{itemize}
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Section 3 - Financial Statement Analysis}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item This section requires the least amount of studying
			\item Remember that DuPont analysis is just the Leverage Ratio, Profit Margin, and Asset Turnover, all of which are on formula sheet
			\item The calculations of these are basic
			\item The following part will have some transactions and you will need to state the impact of the transactions on different ratios
			\item This part has the most "traps" in the exam
			\item Look at the ratio and see which two things affect it. Then work out whether or not the transaction actually impacts it (it may not). If it does, write down it's impact with one sentence of explanation 
		\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Section 4 - Business Taxation \& Audit and Control}

			\item Taxation is hard since it was only covered in one lecture
			\item A company qualifies as a base rate entity if they report an aggregate turnover of less than $50,000,000 AND less than 80\% of its assessable income is base rate entity passive income (eg. from investments)

	\subsection{Section 5 - Multiples Valuation}

		\begin{itemize}
			\item Again, this section is mostly simply calculations
			\item Everything you need is on the formula sheet
			\item Usually the ratios for the comparison companies are already worked out for you
			\item To get an "Implied value" for a company, multiple the ratio of another company by whatever the denominator is for the target company
				\begin{question}{Example}
					Harvey Norman (target company) has an EPS (earnings per share) of \$2.7. Another company had a P/E ratio of 13.14.
					\\ \\
					Since P/E is calculated by
					\begin{align*}
						\text{P/E Ratio} &= \frac{\text{Market Price per share}}{\text{Earnings per share}},
					\end{align*}
					we find the implied Market Price per share of Harvey Norman by
					\begin{align*}
						\text{Implied MP per share} &= \text{P/E} \times \text{EPS}	\\
													&= 13.14 \times 2.7				\\
													&= 35.48 \quad \text{\small(always do 2 decimal places unless specified otherwise)}
					\end{align*}
				\end{question}
			\item They won't ask for analysis on the results since they aren't very valuable without other measurements
		\end{itemize}
